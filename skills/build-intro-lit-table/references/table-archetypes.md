# Introduction literature-table archetypes

Use these archetypes to choose the table's analytical structure. They are distilled from the recurring design logic in the dj01, zz03, zy09, and xq01 projects; adapt them to the focal topic rather than copying their labels.

## Shared architecture

All defensible variants follow the same sequence:

1. Establish the practical management problem.
2. Introduce three to five literature categories in Introduction paragraph 2 or 3.
3. Show breadth through a large Related studies pool in each category.
4. Show depth through several detailed representative examples.
5. Compare the categories' shared explanatory boundary.
6. Derive one umbrella-level general gap.

The table is an evidence structure for the argument. A decorative list of three to five favorable papers per category does not satisfy this architecture.

## Archetype A: Research-stream landscape

Use when the literature contains recognizable research streams, including a method stream outside the focal domain.

Suggested columns:

`Research stream | Related studies | Representative study | Research focus | Empirical/theoretical context | Main purpose/contribution`

Distinctive strengths:

- separates domain knowledge from method adoption;
- makes “the method exists elsewhere but has not answered this domain problem” visible;
- supports a synthesis gap without claiming the method itself is novel.

Risk: streams may sit at different abstraction levels. Write one assignment rule for every stream before collecting papers.

## Archetype B: Perspective–method landscape

Use when several streams propose different solution perspectives for the same management problem.

Suggested columns:

`Category/perspective | Related studies | Representative study | Publication | Methodology/framework | Research content`

Distinctive strengths:

- reveals what existing work optimizes, designs, or improves;
- exposes a shared assumption across otherwise different methods;
- works well when the eventual general gap concerns motivation, strategic choice, or behavioral response rather than operational efficiency.

Risk: method diversity alone is not a gap. The synthesis must identify a shared unresolved management problem.

## Archetype C: Antecedent taxonomy

Use when studies explain an outcome through different classes of antecedents.

Suggested columns:

`Category | Related studies | Representative antecedent | Representative study | Context | Method`

Distinctive strengths:

- makes constructs and levels of analysis easy to compare;
- supports a general gap concerning omitted interaction, temporal process, or contextual boundary;
- works well for cognitive, knowledge, organizational, and environmental antecedents.

Risk: antecedent categories often overlap. State whether assignment follows the paper's primary mechanism, unit of analysis, or focal independent variable.

## Archetype D: Concept–analytical logic landscape

Use when the literature is organized by conceptual explanation rather than a physical table in earlier drafts.

Suggested columns:

`Conceptual lens | Related studies | Representative study | Core explanatory logic | Typical outcome | Analytical limitation`

Distinctive strengths:

- converts a paragraph-based “latent table” into an auditable landscape;
- exposes shared analytical assumptions such as static, net-effect, or single-factor reasoning;
- supports an integration or configurational general gap.

Risk: do not assign the same generic limitation to every paper without reading them. Limitations must be demonstrated at category level and tested against counterexamples.

## Choosing among archetypes

Choose the form by the literature's organization, not by which table looks most sophisticated.

| Literature pattern | Preferred archetype |
|---|---|
| Distinct domain and method streams | A |
| Several solution perspectives/methods address one problem | B |
| Explanations divide into antecedent classes | C |
| Conceptual lenses imply different analytical logics | D |

Hybridize only when one consistent assignment rule still works. If two archetypes require incompatible units of classification, separate them into panels rather than forcing them into one category column.

## Main text versus appendix or supplement

Keep the table in the main text when it is compact: roughly three categories, no more than about twelve representative rows, and five or fewer readable columns. Use an appendix or supplement for wide tables, four or more categories with dense metadata, or long Related studies pools. In all cases, cite and interpret the table in Introduction paragraph 2 or 3.
